Homework 8: Asymptotic Theory & Bayesian Inference

Modes of stochastic convergence, the role of √n, and Bayesian updating

1. Modes of convergence in probability theory

Definitions, implications and roles

A sequence of random variables Xn (all defined on the same probability space (Ω, 𝓕, P)) can approach a limit X (a random variable or a constant) in four main senses. They measure "closeness" in different ways: through single sample paths (a.s.), through the size of the error in expectation (L²), through the probability of large errors at a fixed n (in probability), or only through the shape of the distributions (in distribution).

How they are related

  • a.s. ⇒ in probability and L² ⇒ in probability (by Chebyshev's inequality, P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xn − X)²]/ε²); in probability ⇒ in distribution. So the chain of strength is "in probability" in the middle, with a.s. and L² above it, and in distribution below it. a.s. and L² are not comparable: neither implies the other (counterexamples below).
  • In general nothing goes back. Two partial converses: if the limit is a constant c, convergence in distribution implies convergence in probability; and convergence in probability implies a.s. convergence along a subsequence (also: a.s. convergence plus domination gives L²; in probability plus uniform integrability gives L¹).
  • Tool for a.s. convergence: the first Borel–Cantelli lemma (∑ P(|Xn − X| > ε) < ∞ ⇒ a.s.); for independent events the second one gives the converse (∑ P = ∞ ⇒ the events happen infinitely often a.s.).

The laws of large numbers and the CLT: statements and modes

  • WLLN (Khinchin): Xi i.i.d. with E|X1| < ∞ ⇒ X̄n →P μ. With finite variance the proof is Chebyshev: P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), and X̄n → μ in L² as well, since E[(X̄n − μ)²] = σ²/n.
  • SLLN (Kolmogorov): for i.i.d. Xi, X̄n converges a.s. to a finite limit if and only if E|X1| < ∞, and then the limit is μ. The finite mean is therefore also necessary: for the Cauchy law X̄n has the same Cauchy law for every n and does not converge in any of the four senses.
  • CLT (Lindeberg–Lévy): Xi i.i.d. with finite variance σ² > 0 ⇒ √n (X̄n − μ)/σ →d N(0, 1). It is a statement on the distribution only: the standardized sum does not converge in probability (nor a.s.), because Zn and Z2n stay "far apart" as random variables. For non-identically distributed variables the Lindeberg–Feller conditions replace identical distribution.
  • Other results of statistics built on these modes. Slutsky: Xn →d X and Yn →P c ⇒ Xn + Yn →d X + c, XnYn →d cX (this is how the t-statistic √n(X̄n − μ)/Sn →d N(0,1) is obtained: Sn →P σ by the WLLN, the numerator by the CLT). Continuous mapping and the delta method: √n(g(X̄n) − g(μ)) →d N(0, g′(μ)²σ²). Consistency of an estimator is convergence in probability (often proved with L²: MSE → 0); asymptotic normality of the maximum likelihood estimator is √n(θ̂n − θ0) →d N(0, I(θ0)−1) under regularity conditions. Glivenko–Cantelli: supx |Fn(x) − F(x)| → 0 a.s.

So in the laws the weakest mode (distribution) is used for the shape of the fluctuations (CLT), and the stronger ones for the limit itself: a.s. for a statement on each trajectory (SLLN), in probability for a statement on a fixed large n (WLLN, consistency), L² when the size of the error is of interest.

Why does √n appear everywhere?

For independent X1, …, Xn with variance σ² the variance of the sum is additive, because all the covariances vanish:

Var(Sn) = ∑i Var(Xi) + 2 ∑i<j Cov(Xi, Xj) = nσ²  ⇒  sd(Sn) = σ√n,   sd(X̄n) = σ/√n
  • Estimation and the LLN. The standard error of a mean is σ/√n: to halve the uncertainty the sample size must be multiplied by four. Confidence intervals, sample-size planning and the rate of the WLLN (Chebyshev) all have this form. More generally, regular estimators have √n(θ̂n − θ0) of order one, which is why the "parametric rate" is n−1/2.
  • The CLT. Sn − nμ has spread σ√n and diverges, Sn/n − μ has spread σ/√n and degenerates to 0; Sn − nμ divided by σ√n is the (unique, up to a constant) scaling that keeps the variance equal to 1 and gives a non-degenerate limit, N(0,1). A faster or slower normalization gives a limit that is zero or infinite.
  • Independence is sufficient, not necessary; what is needed is that the covariances are summable. With equicorrelation ρ > 0, Var(Sn) = nσ²(1 + (n − 1)ρ) ∼ ρσ²n²: the variance of X̄n tends to ρσ², not to 0 (a floor), and X̄n does not converge to μ but to the random common component (in L²: to a random variable, not to a constant). With weak dependence (e.g. an AR(1) process with coefficient φ, or any stationary mixing sequence) the covariances decay fast enough, Var(Sn) ∼ nσ²∞ with a long-run variance σ²∞ = σ² + 2∑k≥1 Cov(X0, Xk) (for AR(1) with unit variance, (1 + φ)/(1 − φ)), so √n remains the right scaling, with a different constant: an "effective sample size" n/((1 + φ)/(1 − φ)) replaces n.
  • Finite variance is needed too. For heavy-tailed laws the normalization changes: the sum of n i.i.d. variables with tail index α < 2 must be divided by n1/α and the limit is a stable law (for the Cauchy law, α = 1, the mean X̄n is itself Cauchy for every n).

2. Conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and statistical inference

From conditional probability to Bayes' theorem

Conditional probability. For events A and B with P(B) > 0, P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B): the probability measure is restricted to B and renormalized. In probability theory it is a derived concept (and the basis of independence: A and B are independent iff P(A | B) = P(A)); in statistics it is the language of the model itself, since a statistical model is a family of conditional distributions of the data given the parameter, P(D | θ) (for fixed θ in frequentist inference, where θ is not random; for a random θ in the Bayesian one).

Bayes' theorem follows from writing the intersection in two ways, P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A), and from the law of total probability for a partition {Ai}:

P(Ai | B) = P(B | Ai) P(Ai)∑j P(B | Aj) P(Aj)

For a parameter θ and data D the same formula reads (the sum becomes an integral for a continuous θ):

P(θ | D) = P(D | θ) · P(θ)P(D),   P(D) = ∫ P(D | θ) P(θ) dθ  ⇒  posterior ∝ likelihood × prior
  • Prior P(θ): what is known or assumed about θ before the data (previous studies, expert knowledge, physical constraints, or a deliberately vague choice).
  • Likelihood L(θ) = P(D | θ): the data-generating model seen as a function of θ for the observed D. It is not a probability distribution over θ (it does not integrate to 1) and it measures how well each value of θ explains the data. It is the only ingredient shared with frequentist inference (maximum likelihood).
  • Posterior P(θ | D): the updated distribution of θ. The normalizing constant P(D), the marginal likelihood or evidence, does not depend on θ: it only rescales the product and is the hard part of the computation (hence conjugate priors and MCMC).

Do not confuse P(D | θ) with P(θ | D). P(positive test | disease) and P(disease | positive test) can differ by orders of magnitude (the "base-rate fallacy" of the first example, and the "prosecutor's fallacy" in court). A p-value, for instance, is a probability computed given the null hypothesis, P(data at least as extreme | H0), not the probability that H0 is true.

Why the update is central. The posterior is a complete description of the uncertainty about θ, from which one obtains estimates (posterior mean, median, mode = MAP), credible intervals (P(θ ∈ I | D) = 95% is a direct probability statement about θ, given model and prior; a frequentist confidence interval is a statement on the procedure, not on this θ), tests (posterior probability of hypotheses, Bayes factors) and predictions (the posterior predictive P(new data | D) = ∫ P(new | θ) P(θ | D) dθ, which accounts for the uncertainty on θ). The process is also sequential and coherent: updating on data D1 and then on D2 gives exactly the same posterior as updating once on (D1, D2), so yesterday's posterior is today's prior.

Example 1: the base-rate fallacy (intrusion detection)

A sensor flags intrusions in network traffic. With prior probability of an intrusion π, sensitivity P(alert | intrusion) and specificity P(no alert | no intrusion), the posterior probability of an intrusion given an alert is π·sens / (π·sens + (1 − π)(1 − spec)).

Example 2: the Beta-Binomial model (breach rate of a configuration)

The unknown breach probability θ ∈ [0, 1] of a server configuration has a Beta(α, β) prior; k breaches are observed in n attacks (Binomial likelihood). By conjugacy the posterior is Beta(α + k, β + n − k): the prior acts as α + β "pseudo-trials". The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the data frequency, with weights (α + β) and n:

E[θ | D] = α + kα + β + n = α + βα + β + n · αα + β + nα + β + n · kn

Example 3: sequential updating and the influence of the prior

Data are generated trial by trial from a true θ, and the posterior is updated after every observation for three different priors. Early on the three posteriors disagree (the prior matters); as n grows they merge with the observed frequency and the width of the 95% credible interval shrinks like 1/√n, the same √n of Part 1.

Example 4: other conjugate pairs

Normal mean, known σ (response time of a service). Prior N(μ0, τ²), data mean x̄ of n values with variance σ²: the posterior is normal with precision 1/τ² + n/σ² and mean equal to the average of μ0 and x̄ weighted by the precisions 1/τ² and n/σ². Poisson rate with a Gamma prior (intrusions per day). Prior Gamma(a, b) (rate b), counts c1, …, cn: posterior Gamma(a + ∑ci, b + n), mean (a + ∑ci)/(b + n).

Advantages, limitations and the influence of prior and likelihood

Advantages. (1) Direct probability statements about θ and about hypotheses, and predictions that propagate the uncertainty. (2) Natural regularization: a prior with pseudo-counts stabilizes estimates when the data are few (a rate estimated from 0 breaches in 5 trials is not "exactly 0"; with Beta(2,10) it is 2/17 ≈ 12%). (3) Sequential and coherent updating, so data can arrive in a stream or in several studies. (4) Prior knowledge (earlier studies, physical constraints) enters in a transparent, quantitative way; hierarchical models share strength among related units.

Influence of the prior. The prior acts like α + β additional observations: Example 2 shows that with n = 20 trials and a Beta(2,10) prior, the posterior mean sits between the prior mean 0.167 and the frequency 0.60, while with n = 5000 it is practically the frequency. Example 3 shows that three very different priors give very different conclusions after a few trials and nearly identical ones after a few hundred. Example 1 shows an influence that does not disappear with a good test: when the base rate is very low the prior dominates the conclusion, which is the point of the base-rate fallacy (a 99%-sensitive, 99%-specific sensor at π = 0.1% gives only about a 9% posterior probability after an alert).

Limitations. (1) Subjectivity and sensitivity: the conclusions depend on a prior that must be chosen; "uninformative" is not unique and not invariant under reparametrization (Beta(1,1) vs the Jeffreys Beta(½,½)), so a sensitivity analysis over reasonable priors should accompany the result; a prior in strong conflict with the data pulls the posterior in a misleading direction when n is small, and a prior that assigns zero probability to a value can never be corrected by data. (2) The likelihood is a model: Bayesian conclusions are as good as the data-generating model; with a misspecified likelihood even a large sample gives a confident, wrong posterior. (3) Computation: the evidence P(D) is an integral that is intractable in high dimensions, which requires conjugacy (as here), MCMC or variational approximations. (4) By the Bernstein–von Mises theorem, under regularity conditions (correct and identifiable parametric model, a prior with positive density near the true θ0) the posterior is asymptotically N(θ̂, I(θ̂)−1/n): the data overwhelm the prior, credible intervals and (large-sample) confidence intervals coincide, and Bayesian and frequentist answers agree to first order; this is a statement of convergence in distribution that does not hold in small samples, for infinite-dimensional models in general, or for a misspecified model.
