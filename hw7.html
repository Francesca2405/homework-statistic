Homework 7: Statistical Independence

Contingency tables, expected frequencies & Pearson's chi-square test

Theoretical framework: independence, association and causality

1. Independence in probability, in descriptive statistics, and in inference

In probability, independence is a property of a model: two random variables X and Y are independent if the joint distribution is the product of the marginals, P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) · P(Y = y) for all (x, y); equivalently P(Y = y | X = x) = P(Y = y) whenever P(X = x) > 0.

In descriptive statistics, the same idea is applied to a dataset: the variables are statistically independent in the data if the joint relative frequencies are the product of the marginal relative frequencies, fij = fi· f·j for every cell. This is equivalent to three other statements: the conditional distributions of Y are identical in every row, the conditional distributions of X are identical in every column, and the observed counts equal the expected counts, Oij = Eij, i.e. χ² = 0. In a real sample this exact equality almost never holds: the table is "more or less" independent, and χ² measures how far it is.

In inferential statistics, the dataset is a random sample, and the question becomes whether the observed departure from independence can be explained by sampling variability alone. The null hypothesis H0 states that the two variables are independent in the population; under H0 the statistic χ² is still positive but, for large enough expected counts, approximately χ² distributed (Section 3).

2. Contingency tables, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected counts

Given the pairs (xk, fk) for k = 1, …, N, the contingency table counts Oij = number of pairs with X = xi and F = fj. With row totals Ri and column totals Cj:

  • joint relative frequency fij = Oij/N; marginals fi· = Ri/N and f·j = Cj/N;
  • conditional frequencies f(F = fj | X = xi) = Oij/Ri and f(X = xi | F = fj) = Oij/Cj.

Under independence the expected count of the cell (i, j) is the count that the marginals would produce if each row had the same conditional distribution:

Eij = N fi· f·j = Ri · CjN

3. Pearson's chi-square statistic, degrees of freedom and p-value

χ² = ∑i ∑j (Oij − Eij)²Eij,    df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

The degrees of freedom are (r − 1)(c − 1) because, once the margins are fixed, only that many cells can vary freely. Under H0, χ² is approximately χ²df-distributed, so the p-value is P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs) and H0 is rejected at level α when p < α, equivalently when χ²obs exceeds the critical value. Not rejecting H0 means that the data are compatible with independence, not that independence has been proved. The approximation needs the expected counts not to be too small (a common rule: all Eij ≥ 5, or at most 20% of the cells below 5 and none below 1) and observations that are independent of one another. For 2 × 2 tables with small counts, Yates' continuity correction or Fisher's exact test are used; a permutation test, which does not rely on the χ² approximation, is also shown below.

4. Strength of the association

χ² grows with the sample size (multiplying all counts by 10 multiplies χ² by 10 and leaves the association unchanged), so a small p-value says that an association is detectable, not that it is large. The usual effect size is Cramér's V = √(χ² / (N · min(r − 1, c − 1))), which lies in [0, 1] (0 = independence). With w = V·√(min(r−1, c−1)), Cohen's conventional thresholds are 0.1 (small), 0.3 (medium), 0.5 (large). For 2 × 2 tables the odds ratio ad/(bc) is also reported; OR = 1 means independence. Adjusted standardized residuals (O − E)/√(E(1 − Ri/N)(1 − Cj/N)) show which cells are responsible: they are approximately standard normal under H0, so |residual| > 2 marks a cell that deviates noticeably.

5. Association vs. causality

A small p-value indicates statistical association: knowing the category of X changes the distribution of F. It does not say that X causes F. The association may be produced by a confounding variable that affects both (e.g. the size of an organization affects both its security measures and its exposure to attacks), by reverse causation (organizations adopt measures after a breach), by selection (who attends tutoring is not random), by aggregating over groups (Simpson's paradox), or by chance. Establishing causality requires a design that controls these alternatives (randomization, controlling for confounders, temporal precedence) and a plausible mechanism, none of which a bivariate table provides.

Discussion

How to read the results

Descriptive reading. The conditional frequencies are the key: if the variables were independent in the data, all the rows of the "F given X" table would be equal to the marginal row, and the stacked bars of Figure 1 would line up with the dashed guides. The more they differ, the larger χ². The residual map (Figure 2) says which cells make the difference, and V and the odds ratio say how large the difference is.

Inferential reading. χ² is compared with the χ²df distribution (Figure 3) or, without relying on that approximation, with the distribution obtained by shuffling the F labels while keeping the margins fixed (Figure 4: if X and F were unrelated, every shuffle of F would be an equally likely dataset). The two p-values agree when the expected counts are large; when they differ, the permutation or Fisher value is the safer one.

The datasets in this page

Firewall and breach (synthetic). The counts are invented to illustrate a very strong association: with an active firewall 4.3% of the 470 systems were breached, with the firewall disabled 65.2% of 230. Even so, the table does not show that the firewall prevents breaches. Larger or better funded organizations may both keep the firewall active and have better monitoring; organizations breached in the past may disable or reconfigure systems during an incident (or enable the firewall only afterwards); a disabled firewall may be a symptom of a poorly managed network that has other weaknesses. A causal claim would need a controlled comparison, for instance randomizing the firewall setting among comparable machines.

Tutoring and exam (synthetic). 80% of the students who attended passed, against 40% of those who did not: a medium-sized association by Cohen's thresholds (V ≈ 0.41). It cannot be read as the effect of tutoring because students choose to attend: motivated or better prepared students may be more likely both to attend and to pass (selection and confounding), and the effect of tutoring itself is not separable from them.

Titanic (real data, Dawson 1995; the Titanic table of R's datasets package). The survival rate falls from first class (62%) to third class (25%) and crew (24%). The association is highly significant, but class is entangled with sex, age and the position of the cabins; part of the dependence is due to the "women and children first" policy and to the composition of each class, which is why a table of class against survival alone cannot isolate the effect of class.

Independent variables (simulated). Both variables are drawn independently, so the null hypothesis is true by construction. With α = 0.05 a test of a true null hypothesis rejects in about 5% of the datasets: try other seeds; failing to reject is the typical outcome, and the occasional rejection is exactly the type I error rate.
