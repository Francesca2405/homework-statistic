Homework 4: Stochastic Penetration Models

Law of Large Numbers (LLN) & Central Limit Theorem (CLT) simulations

1. The simulation engine

Each of the m attackers moves through n systems. On system i the attacker obtains a score Xi; the increments are i.i.d. and the attacker's aggregate result is the random walk Sk = X₁ + … + Xk. The attackers are independent copies of the same walk. Two models are compared: Bernoulli (Xi ∈ {0,1}, P(1) = p, mean μ = p, variance σ² = p(1−p)) and the symmetric Rademacher model (Xi = ±1 with probability ½, μ = 0, σ² = 1). 2. Behaviour as n increases

The homework asks how the three quantities change with n, so this study reuses the model chosen above and simulates ms attackers on a single walk of 2000 steps, reading the score at n = 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000. Three things are measured: the variance of Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n against their theoretical values (nσ², σ²/n, σ²); the frequency of large deviations P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) against the Chebyshev and Hoeffding bounds, which is exactly convergence in probability; and the Kolmogorov distance between the law of the standardized score and N(0,1).

3. What the number of attackers m changes

For one attacker the law of Sn does not depend on m; m only decides how well the simulated sample describes it. To measure this, the experiment below fixes n = 100, estimates Var(Sn/√n) from m attackers, and repeats the whole estimate 40 times for each m. 4. Interpretation and discussion

The roles of n and m

n is the size of the attack surface: the number of systems an attacker goes through, hence the length of the walk. It changes the distribution of each attacker's result. m is the number of attackers observed, hence the sample from which we estimate that distribution; it does not change the law of one attacker's score, only the accuracy of the picture (Section 3: error of order 1/√m). If instead one adds up the results of all attackers, T = Σj Sn(j), this is a sum of mn i.i.d. increments, with mean mnμ and variance mnσ²: its relative fluctuation shrinks as 1/√(mn), so the defender facing many attackers on many systems sees a more predictable aggregate, while in the zero-mean case the typical size of the aggregate still grows like √(mn).

The table is what Figure 2 (left) confirms: √n is the only scaling for which the variance neither blows up nor vanishes, and this is why a non-degenerate limit law can exist.

Law of Large Numbers

The Weak Law is a statement on probabilities, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0. For these bounded variables it can be proved in one line: Chebyshev gives P ≤ σ²/(nε²) and Hoeffding gives the much sharper 2·exp(−2nε²/(b−a)²), with b−a = 1 (Bernoulli) or 2 (Rademacher). The simulation does not prove this; it illustrates it, and Figure 2 (middle) shows how far below Chebyshev's bound the real probability lies. The funnel in the trajectory plot for the average shows the same fact path by path (each walk settles on μ, a statement the Strong Law makes precise), but it is the probability of deviations, not the picture, that expresses convergence in probability. A sustained attack therefore has a success rate that is close to p, with an uncertainty that decreases as σ/√n and never reaches zero for finite n.

Central Limit Theorem

For i.i.d. increments with finite variance, (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to N(0,1). In the Rademacher case μ = 0 and σ = 1, so this is exactly Sn/√n. In the Bernoulli case Sn/√n has mean p√n, which diverges, so it cannot converge to a standard normal: the centered and scaled version must be used (this is the "standardized" option). Convergence is only asymptotic: because Sn takes values on a lattice, the Kolmogorov distance from N(0,1) cannot be smaller than about half the largest probability jump, which is of order 1/√n (Berry-Esseen); it is larger for p far from ½ because of the skewness of Bernoulli sums. Histograms here use bins aligned with the lattice, so that the jagged look of an histogram is not an artefact of the bin width. The dependence of the plotted trajectories on k should also be read with care: at each fixed k the value Sk/√k is approximately N(0,1), but along k the path wanders (the law of the iterated logarithm says it is not bounded); the "scaled walk" option shows the object that converges as a process, a Brownian motion, with a band of width proportional to √t.

Limits of this experiment

A simulation can only show behaviour for finite n and m and for the chosen p; for p very close to 0 or 1 the normal approximation needs a much larger n (a common rule of thumb is np(1−p) ≳ 10). The results change with the seed within the sampling error described in Section 3. The model assumes independent identical systems and attackers who do not learn: with dependence between systems or with attackers who adapt, the variances and the limit laws above would no longer hold.
