Homework 2: Cryptography & Cryptanalysis

Toy ECC on secp256k1, the Caesar cipher, and attacks exploiting statistics and small key spaces

Link with Homework 1. Part 1 studied the curve y² = x³ + 7 over F17 with G = (1, 5), a subgroup of order 9 where every key can be listed by hand, and verified exhaustively that (k₁+k₂)G = k₁G + k₂G. This homework keeps the same equation but uses the real secp256k1 parameters, so the only thing left to "shrink" is the private key. The homomorphism checked in Part 1 is what makes ElGamal decryption work and, as section 3 shows, also what leaks the plaintext when the ephemeral key is reused. Part 2 showed that an Ethereum address is the hash of the public key Q = kG: a private key drawn from a tiny range would therefore fall to the same brute force.

1. Encryption: toy ECC (secp256k1) and Caesar

The ECC scheme is an ElGamal-style encryption (in its "lifted" form, where the message is encoded in the exponent) over the real secp256k1 curve y² = x³ + 7 over Fp, p = 2²⁵⁶ − 2³² − 977, with the standard generator G of prime order n ≈ 2²⁵⁶. All arithmetic uses BigInt. The only "toy" aspect is that Alice's private key k is restricted to [1, 2b] so that attacks become feasible. Each character (code 1 to 255) is encoded as the point M = cG. The same message is encrypted with both schemes (the Caesar cipher shifts letters only and leaves spaces and punctuation unchanged). Messages are limited to 100 characters, and characters outside Latin-1 are replaced by "?".

2. Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

The attacker learns letter statistics from a corpus of English text: unigram frequencies and bigram conditional log-probabilities, with add-one smoothing. Three scores are computed for each of the 26 shifts and the best one wins: the χ² statistic against the expected unigram counts, the unigram log-likelihood, and the bigram log-likelihood. Together with printing all 26 decryptions (exhaustive key search, which a human then reads) these are the three approaches compared in the optional part.

3. Attacking the toy ECC with a restricted key space

Run section 1 first: the attacker sees Alice's public key Q = kG and the ciphertext. Four attacks are available. Exhaustive search computes G, 2G, 3G, … until Q appears (about 2b group operations; it uses Jacobian coordinates, so no field inversion is needed per step). Baby-step giant-step needs about 2·2b/2 operations and 2b/2 memory. The known-plaintext attack needs no private key when r is reused. The ciphertext-only attack needs neither k nor any known plaintext: with a reused r, C₂ = (c + rk)G is a Caesar cipher over the group, so differences of ciphertexts reveal differences of characters.

4. Discussion

Steps of the ECC algorithm and the role of the keys

Key generation: Alice picks a private key k and publishes Q = kG. Encryption (Bob): he encodes a character as M = cG, picks a fresh ephemeral secret r, and sends C₁ = rG, C₂ = M + rQ. Decryption (Alice): kC₁ = rkG = rQ, hence M = C₂ − kC₁, and the character is read from a table of cG for all 255 values of c. The public key lets anyone encrypt, the private key is the only trapdoor to decrypt, and the ephemeral key makes the encryption randomized (it must never be reused). Security rests on the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP): recovering k from Q and G. With a fresh r per block the scheme is semantically secure under the decisional Diffie-Hellman assumption in the group; without that assumption, "looks random" is only a heuristic.

The "Algebraic view" row is the bridge between the two halves of the homework. With a reused r the toy ECC is a Caesar cipher whose "alphabet" is a group of order n. Making the key space astronomically larger (2²⁵⁶ shifts instead of 26) does not help, because the structure of a shift cipher is what leaks: the difference of two ciphertext symbols, (cᵢ + t)G − (cⱼ + t)G = (cᵢ − cⱼ)G, does not depend on the key at all.

Effectiveness and limits of the statistical attack

The attack works because the Caesar cipher only permutes letters, so the ciphertext keeps the shape of the language. With 1000 trials the 95% confidence interval is at most about ±3 percentage points, and the intervals are drawn in Figure 2c.

The bigram score uses more information per letter than a unigram score, so it needs fewer letters, but it needs a good corpus and a language known in advance. Below a handful of letters no scorer can be reliable, because several shifts give plausible English (the success rate has a ceiling below 100% for very short texts). Exhaustive key search is the limit case: with 26 keys one can print all candidates and read them, and the three scores just automate that choice. The attack degrades with a different language or domain than the corpus (the model here is trained on novels), with texts of unusual statistics, and when the text is too short; note also that the page strips spaces before scoring, but a real attacker would also use the visible word lengths. Attacks on the toy ECC and why they do not scale

The attacks in section 3 use no property of the language: the key-recovery ones only exploit that k lies in a small set. Exhaustive search costs about 2b steps, and baby-step giant-step reduces this to about 2·2b/2 at the price of memory (Pollard's rho achieves the same time with negligible memory). For a properly configured curve the private key is uniform in [1, n−1] with n ≈ 2²⁵⁶, so exhaustive search needs about 2²⁵⁶ operations, and even the best generic algorithms need about √n ≈ 2¹²⁸. At 10¹⁸ group operations per second that is of the order of 10¹³ years, hundreds of times the age of the universe. Figure 3b shows the gap: the curves are straight lines in b, so each extra key bit doubles the exhaustive cost and multiplies the BSGS cost by √2. This is why the restricted key space, not the curve, is what we break. The curve of Part 1 is the extreme case: its subgroup has 9 elements, so every attack there is immediate.

Which weaknesses come from the key space and which from the design?

Key space: exhaustive search and BSGS (section 3, first button). Design of the toy scheme (these remain even with a full-size key): (1) reusing r turns the scheme into a shift cipher on the group: C₂ becomes a fixed substitution of the plaintext, repeated characters leak, a single known plaintext character reveals rQ and decrypts everything without k (second button), and even with no known plaintext the differences C₂[i] − C₂[0] = (c[i] − c[0])G recover the message up to one unknown shift, which a language score resolves (third button); two ciphertexts with the same r also reveal M₁ − M₂. (2) The deterministic encoding c → cG works only because the message space has 255 values: decrypting an arbitrary number would require solving the discrete logarithm, so a real scheme uses a proper embedding or, better, ECDH plus a symmetric cipher. (3) ElGamal is malleable (an attacker can add a known point to C₂ and change the plaintext in a controlled way, and the lifted form is additively homomorphic by construction) and offers no authentication. (4) Points received are not checked to lie on the curve, which is the basis of invalid-curve attacks, since the addition formulas do not use the constant 7. Weak keys are also a real-world problem: it is widely reported that Ethereum addresses derived from tiny private keys are emptied by bots within moments of receiving funds.

Against the Caesar cipher the attacker exploits information that leaks from the plaintext (language statistics), so accuracy grows with message length and needs a corpus. Against the restricted ECC the key-recovery attacks exploit how few keys exist: the cost depends only on the key size, not on the message, and the algorithm is the same for any plaintext. The two reused-r attacks are different again: they do not look for the key, they exploit that the scheme, used badly, is just a Caesar-like substitution, and frequency analysis of the blocks, as in section 2, applies to them as well.
