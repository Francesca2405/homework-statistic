Homework 5: Sampling & Welford's Algorithm

Welford's recurrence formulas & inferential statistics

1. Welford's recurrence formulas

An online algorithm updates its result one observation at a time, without storing the data. Welford's recurrences do this for the sum of squares SS and the variance, in the same way as Knuth's recurrence does for the mean. We derive them step by step.

Notation. Given x1, …, xn:

Mn = (1/n) ∑i=1..n xi,   SSn = ∑i=1..n (xi − Mn)²,   Sn² = SSn / (n − 1)  (n ≥ 2),   δn = xn − Mn−1

Step 1: the mean (Knuth)

Isolate the new observation and use the previous mean: n Mn = ∑i<n xi + xn = (n−1) Mn−1 + xn. Dividing by n:

Mn = Mn−1 + (xn − Mn−1) / n = Mn−1 + δn / n

Two consequences are used below: (a) Mn − Mn−1 = δn/n; (b) xn − Mn = δn − δn/n = (n−1) δn/n.

Step 2: the sum of squares (same approach)

Again isolate the n-th term and express everything through the previous step:

SSn = ∑i<n (xi − Mn)² + (xn − Mn)²

In the first sum add and subtract Mn−1 and use (a): xi − Mn = (xi − Mn−1) − δn/n. Expanding the square,

∑i<n (xi − Mn)² = ∑i<n (xi − Mn−1)² − 2 (δn/n) ∑i<n (xi − Mn−1) + (n−1) δn²/n²

The first sum is SSn−1, and the middle sum is zero because the deviations from the mean Mn−1 of the first n−1 points sum to zero. So the first sum equals SSn−1 + (n−1) δn²/n². By (b) the last term is (xn − Mn)² = (n−1)² δn²/n². Adding the two pieces,

SSn = SSn−1 + δn² [(n−1) + (n−1)²] / n² = SSn−1 + (n−1) δn² / n

Using (b) once more, (n−1) δn²/n = δn (xn − Mn), which gives Welford's form:

SSn = SSn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)(xn − Mn)

Step 3: the variance

Dividing SSn = SSn−1 + (n−1) δn²/n by n−1 and using SSn−1 = (n−2) Sn−1²:

Sn² = ((n − 2)/(n − 1)) Sn−1² + δn² / n   (n ≥ 3),   S2² = δ2² / 2

Initial conditions. M0 = 0 and SS0 = 0. For n = 1 the formulas give M1 = x1 and SS1 = 0 (the factor n−1 vanishes), as it must be. The variance is only defined from n = 2.

The update uses only the previous values Mn−1, SSn−1 and the new point: constant memory, one pass, and the increment δn(xn − Mn) is never negative (both factors have the same sign), so there is no subtraction of two large, almost equal numbers.

Implementation

The core of the algorithm is three lines (the third one is the update of SS; n is the number of points seen so far):

function welford(data) { let n = 0, mean = 0, SS = 0; for (const x of data) { n++; const delta = x - mean; // δ_n = x_n - M_(n-1) mean += delta / n; // M_n = M_(n-1) + δ_n / n SS += delta * (x - mean); // SS_n = SS_(n-1) + δ_n (x_n - M_n) } return { n, mean, variance: n > 1 ? SS / (n - 1) : NaN }; }

Why not the textbook formula?

The "shortcut" formula (∑x² − (∑x)²/n)/(n−1) is also one-pass, but it subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers when the data have a large mean compared with their spread, and loses all precision. The experiment below uses 1000 integer scores in {0,…,10} (true variance close to 10) shifted by a constant. Welford's algorithm and the two-pass formula agree to many digits, while the shortcut formula breaks down as the offset grows.

2. Sampling mean and variance with Welford

This extends Homework 4: there m attackers went through n systems with a score of 0/1 or ±1. Here every attempt receives an integer score from 0 to 10 drawn from a fixed distribution. Note that the Binomial B(10, p) is exactly the sum of 10 Bernoulli(p) trials of Homework 4. Each of the m hackers attacks n systems, so each trajectory has n scores, and Welford's formulas give that hacker's sample mean and sample variance on the fly. Then the m sample means and the m sample variances are averaged and compared with the true μ and σ².

3. Repeating the experiment for different n

The same experiment is repeated with n = 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000 and ms hackers each (independent runs). For each n it records the average of the estimates (bias) and the standard deviation of the estimates across hackers (variability), against their theoretical values: σ/√n for the sample mean and σ²√(κ/n + 2/(n−1)) for the sample variance, where κ is the excess kurtosis of the score distribution. 4. Conclusions

Comparing the sample averages with μ and σ²

In Section 2 the average of the m sample means is statistically indistinguishable from μ, and the average of the m sample variances (divisor n−1) from σ²: the differences Δ are of the size of their standard errors (the z-scores in the table stay of order 1), whatever the distribution and even for n = 2 (Section 3). The two averages estimate the expected values of the estimators, so the simulation illustrates that the sample mean and the sample variance are unbiased estimators: E[Mn] = μ by linearity and E[Sn²] = σ². For the second, E[∑(xi − Mn)²] = E[∑(xi − μ)²] − n E[(Mn − μ)²] = nσ² − σ² = (n−1)σ². This also explains the "Bessel correction": dividing by n gives expected value σ²(n−1)/n, and the table shows a clearly negative z-score for it, which disappears only for large n. A simulation cannot prove unbiasedness, it can only show that no bias larger than the standard error is visible. Note also that unbiasedness is not inherited by transformations: the sample standard deviation S has expected value slightly below σ (Jensen's inequality), as the table shows.

Why this is useful in inferential statistics

In practice only one sample is available (one hacker's trajectory), and μ and σ² are unknown. Unbiasedness says that the estimates from that single sample are centered on the truth, with no systematic over- or underestimation, for every sample size. The sample variance then provides an estimate of the standard error of the mean, S/√n, which is the basis of confidence intervals and of the t-test for the mean. Because the sample mean is approximately normal with variance σ²/n (CLT, see the histogram), the interval Mn ± t·S/√n covers μ with the desired probability. Welford's formulas make this possible while the data are still arriving, with constant memory, for instance on a stream of attack attempts.

The effect of the sample size n

Unbiasedness holds for every n ≥ 2 and does not depend on n, but the variability does: Var(Mn) = σ²/n and Var(Sn²) = σ⁴(κ/n + 2/(n−1)), so both standard deviations decrease like 1/√n (the slope −½ of Figure 3, left). The decrease is real but not "dramatic": to halve the spread of the sample means the sample size must be multiplied by four. The relative precision of the variance depends on the shape of the distribution through κ: for the same n, the uniform score (κ = −1.22) gives a more precise S² than a heavy-tailed score would. The distribution of the sample means also becomes narrower and closer to a normal curve (Figure 2, left); the distribution of the sample variances is skewed for small n. This is the statement of consistency: the estimates converge to the true parameters as n grows, which is a stronger and different property than unbiasedness. The m hackers do not change the accuracy of any one hacker: m only improves the accuracy of this Monte Carlo experiment (standard errors shrink like 1/√m), as in Homework 4.
